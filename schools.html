01 Safety & safeguarding

The questions every parent asks, answered properly.

This is the part most event pages gloss over. Here is the whole picture — including the things we promise not to do. If your school’s safeguarding lead asks a question that isn’t answered here, email us and we will answer in writing before you sign up.

How much live contact there is

Build Day runs asynchronously by default. There is no requirement for any student to sit in a live one-to-one video call with an adult they have never met.

  • Monday’s kickoff and Friday’s showcase are broadcast sessions — one-to-many, moderated, and optional.
  • There is no unmoderated small-group video chat at any point in the week.
  • Day-to-day help happens in a written thread, so a parent can read exactly what was said.
  • Teachers are welcome to watch every live session, and we tell them the joining link in advance.

Who can see a student’s game

This is the one that matters most, so it is a default rather than a setting you have to remember to switch on.

  • Submissions are unlisted by default — a game plays only for people holding the link. It is not searchable and not on any public page.
  • We do not publish student work to Roblox’s public site. A game is only made public if the student or their teacher explicitly chooses to publish it.
  • Showcase entries are labelled with a first name and initial, or a Roblox display name if the student prefers.
  • Students can withdraw an entry at any time by asking, and we take it down the same day.

What data we hold

  • Registration collects a name, an email, an age band and — optionally — a school name. That is all.
  • We never ask for a Roblox password, and nobody on the team needs one. If anyone messages a student asking for it, it is a scam — report it and block.
  • We do not ask students to add us on Roblox, accept a friend request, or join a group.
  • Registration data is deleted 90 days after the showcase, on request, or whenever a school asks.
  • Photos are only taken at the showcase, and only with written consent. The default is no photography.

Students under 13

Build Day is open to under-13s with a parent or guardian’s permission. Roblox’s own age rules apply in full, and they are genuinely protective — this is not a workaround.

  • A parent or guardian signs the consent form, or a teacher signs under your school’s policy for a whole class cohort.
  • Under-13 Roblox accounts have direct messages, voice chat and group chat disabled at the platform level — not merely switched off for our week.
  • Teams can work entirely in written messages. Nothing in Build Day requires a student to speak to another student.
  • We send a short parent summary on the Friday of your school’s chosen week, so a parent is not hearing about it second-hand.

Reporting and escalation

  • On day one we show every student how to use Roblox’s own block and report tools, and we show them where the report goes.
  • Our designated safeguarding contact is name, role, email — published to schools, not hidden behind a contact form.
  • We acknowledge any safeguarding report within one school day and action it the same day where a child may be at risk.
  • Schools keep their own reporting duty. We support it, and we do not ask a school to hand over that responsibility.

What we do not do

The commitments below are the reason we can be used in a school at all.

  • No cold direct messages to students, and no unsolicited friend requests.
  • No student-facing voice chat we control.
  • We never ask for payment details, Robux, gift cards, or a premium upgrade. These are the exact levers used to scam children on Roblox.
  • We never ask a student to run a script, install an application, or add a friend that we (or another participant) sent them.
  • We do not sell, rent, or share student data. There is no advertising in any Build Day surface.

02 Time commitment & cost

Homework-shaped, not school-shaped.

One school week. Two lessons, or roughly forty-five minutes most evenings. The realistic total is a little over four hours per student — which is why it survives contact with a real timetable.

Suggested minute-by-minute plan for a class running Build Day
Day What students do Suggested time
Monday Read the theme, pick an idea, sketch a build plan. Optional kickoff stream. 20 min (+20 optional)
Tuesday Parts, colours, terrain and getting something moving. First line of Luau. 45 min
Wednesday Scripting, playtesting with the team, fixing what broke. 45 min
Thursday Playtest, polish, and react to the mid-week surprise twist. 45 min
Friday Publish, submit the link, record a sixty-second pitch. Showcase. 30 min (+20 showcase)
Total Plus whatever a student wants to spend making it better. ~4 hrs

Run it take-home

Most of your students already play Roblox at home and already own a machine that can run Studio. Build Day then costs you one kickoff lesson and zero supervision — which is also the most common way it gets run.

1 lesson to launch

Run it in class

Two lessons a week is the comfortable minimum. Teachers usually take the Monday kickoff and the Friday showcase, and leave the middle three days as the build window.

2 lessons + homework

Shorter version

No week to spare? Run the theme and the build, skip the kickoff and the showcase. The learning value is almost entirely in the building, not the ceremony.

No live sessions

Heads up The Chromebook question

Roblox Studio runs on Windows and macOS. It does not run on ChromeOS. A Chromebook-only cohort cannot build during a lesson, and no amount of planning changes that. Three honest options:

  • Take-home. Send it home and run the theme and showcase only. Most students will have access to a Windows or Mac machine already.
  • Borrow a device. A handful of school Windows laptops, rotated through the class across the three build days.
  • Do the non-building outcomes. The theme, the build plan and the sixty-second pitch all work on a Chromebook and carry most of the learning value.

We would rather tell you this now than have a teacher find out on Tuesday.

03 Learning outcomes

What a student can actually do on Friday that they couldn’t on Monday.

Learning outcomes written as capabilities, not topics — because a student who has “done scripting” has not necessarily learned anything. Formal curriculum mapping is available on request (add your framework, e.g. KS3 Computing, Common Core).

01

Computational thinking

Take a vague creative brief, break it into parts, decide what to build first, discover what is actually hard, and cut scope. This is the core habit, and it is the one students carry into every other subject.

02

Reading and writing code

Variables, functions, events and conditionals in Luau — Roblox’s language. Plus the unglamorous half: reading an error message and working out which line it is complaining about.

03

Spatial and 3D reasoning

Working in three dimensions where there is no correct answer: scale, proportion, camera angles, lighting, and why a room feels wrong before you can say why.

04

Interface and interaction design

Menus, HUD, feedback, and the difference between a control that works and one a player can guess. A genuinely cross-curricular outcome that computer science courses tend to under-teach.

05

Iteration and resilience

Build, break, fix, repeat — usually in a team, usually under a deadline. The explicit message of the week is that a rough working thing beats a perfect unfinished one, which is a lesson about adult life too.

06

Collaboration

Splitting a build between people, agreeing who is doing what, and reviewing each other’s work. Teams can be 1–5, so a solo student gets the planning discipline without needing a group.

07

Explaining your own work

A sixty-second pitch, recorded. Design decisions defended out loud in language that is not code — a genuinely hard skill, and a comfortable alternative for students who find presenting difficult.

08

Digital citizenship

Permissions, unlisted versus public, reporting and blocking, and how to spot a scam — practised in the environment where students actually meet risk, not on a worksheet about it.

09

A finished, assessable artifact

Three things a teacher can actually mark if they want to: a playable link, a written build plan, and a recorded pitch. Not a screenshot portfolio, and not a certificate — evidence of the work.

Cross-curricular links: computing, design & technology, art & design, English (oracy and pitching), mathematics (scale, coordinates, ratio), and PSHE or citizenship (digital safety).

04 Benefits

Why a school would actually run this.

Not the brochure version. These are the reasons that survive a conversation with a head of department and a finance officer.

Zero cost, zero procurement

No licence, no seat count, no purchase order, no IT department project. Roblox Studio is a free download, which removes the single biggest reason creative computing projects die.

No budget line

Reaches non-coders

Students who would opt out of a programming course often stay in for this, because the entry point is a creative brief rather than a syntax lesson. The ones who arrive already fluent get stretched instead.

Lower entry barrier

Low teacher preparation

We supply the theme, the day-by-day schedule, a printable build plan and a consent form. You supply a lesson slot and an encouraging word. There is no coursework to mark in advance.

Under an hour of prep

Easy differentiation

Solo or teams of five, with stretch and support prompts in the plan. Students who finish early can add a second mechanic; students who struggle can submit a plan and a pitch and still take the same outcomes.

Solo or teams of 5

The showcase does your marketing

A playable demo from your students is a very good thing to show at a parents’ evening, an assembly, or an open day — and the students presenting it are the most persuasive ambassadors you could possibly have.

Assembly-ready

Supports your digital-literacy policy

Online safety, permissions, reporting and stranger-danger are practised in the place students actually encounter them. That is easier to evidence in a monitoring cycle than a worksheet.

05 Requirements & access

What a student needs, and what they don’t.

They need

  • A Windows or macOS computer — desktop or laptop. Around 1 GB free disk.
  • A free Roblox account. It does not need Robux or premium.
  • Roblox Studio, a free download. We send a setup link and a five-minute install guide.
  • An internet connection, and headphones if they want them.
  • A parent or guardian’s permission if they are under 13.

They do not need

  • Any coding or computer science background. Genuinely none.
  • A powerful or expensive machine. A five-year-old laptop is enough.
  • Robux, premium, or any in-game purchase. We never ask for money.
  • A microphone. Every team activity works in writing.
  • To work in a team. Solo is a first-class way to do Build Day.

Access and inclusion

  • Roblox Studio is fully keyboard operable, and the week’s tasks do not rely on fast mouse work.
  • No task requires a specific colour, a specific reaction time, or spoken participation.
  • Students who cannot commit to the full week can submit a build plan and a written pitch instead, and take the same learning outcomes.
  • Extended submissions are available for students with a deadline that should not move — ask, and it moves.
  • Nothing in the week costs money, so a student without a home computer or a paid account is not disadvantaged.
